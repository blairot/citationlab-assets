The Citation Audit

A protocol for finding out whether AI search knows your brand exists.

From @thecitationlab

Most "AI visibility audits" are a vendor running their own tool and selling you the output. This is the manual version. It takes about 40 minutes, costs nothing, and you own the result.

It will tell you three things: whether you get cited at all, which competitors get cited instead, and whether your problem is your site or the rest of the internet.

That third one is the important one, and almost nobody checks it.

Before you start

  • A spreadsheet
  • 40 minutes
  • Logged-out or incognito sessions for every engine. Personalisation will quietly corrupt your results and you'll never notice.

One warning up front: this measures a moving target. Google switched AI Overviews to Gemini 3 on 27 January 2026 and citation behaviour shifted noticeably within days. A result from three months ago is history, not data.

Step 1 — Write 5 buyer prompts

Not keywords. Prompts. The sentence a real buyer types when they don't know your brand yet.

Good:

  • "best project management tool for a 12-person agency"
  • "alternatives to [competitor] for teams that hate timesheets"
  • "what should I look for in a [category] vendor"

Bad:

  • Your brand name. That tests recall, not discovery, and it will flatter you.
  • Anything with your product's exact positioning language in it. You'll get cited and learn nothing.

The test is only as honest as the prompts. Write them before you look at any results.

Step 2 — Run each prompt 10 times

Per engine: ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Gemini.

New session every single time. Not a new message in the same chat. These models carry context and will start agreeing with themselves, which produces a beautiful, consistent, completely fake result.

50 runs per engine. It's tedious. That tedium is the moat — it's why nobody has your data.

Step 3 — Log every run

Prompt Engine Run # Brands named (in order) You cited? Linked? Source domains

Record every brand named, in order. Order matters more than people assume — first-named is disproportionately what gets clicked.

Track "cited" and "linked" separately. Being mentioned in prose without a link is worth far less, and a lot of tools conflate the two.

Step 4 — Read it

Four outcomes. Find yours.

Never named in 50 runs. You have a consensus problem, not a product problem. The model isn't ranking products — it's summarising what the internet says about products, and the internet isn't saying anything about you. No amount of on-site work fixes this. Go to Step 5.

Named occasionally, never first. You exist but you're not the default. Usually a depth problem: you appear in a few roundups, competitors appear in dozens.

Named only on prompts containing your positioning language. You're visible to people already halfway to buying you, and invisible to everyone earlier. This is the most common result for well-run marketing teams and the easiest to mistake for success.

Named consistently, cited with links. Genuinely good. Re-test quarterly and watch for erosion, because that's what the trend data says is coming.

Step 5 — Fixes, in order of actual leverage

Ordered by return, not by how enjoyable they are.

  1. Get into third-party roundups. The "best X for Y" listicles you don't control. Every consistently-cited brand we've looked at appears in several. This is the highest-leverage and least popular item on the list, because it's outreach and everyone would rather write a blog post.

  2. Put your pricing on a public URL. Structured, parseable, no "contact sales" wall. Models cite what they can read.

  3. Get the grounding sources right. Wikipedia, G2, Crunchbase, category directories. These are what the engines reach for when they need a fact they can stand behind.

  4. Write the comparison page yourself. You vs the alternatives, honestly. It gets cited more than your homepage. Yes, including where you lose.

  5. Then do the on-site work. Schema, clean headings, direct answers near the top. It matters, but it's fifth for a reason, and most agencies sell it first because it's the part they can bill for.

Step 6 — Re-test

Quarterly at minimum. Immediately after any major model release.

Keep every run. The single most valuable thing you'll own in 12 months is a time series nobody else bothered to collect.

What this doesn't tell you

Worth being straight about the limits.

  • 50 runs is enough to see a pattern, not enough for statistical significance. Treat 20% and 25% as the same number.
  • It measures citation, not traffic or revenue. The relationship between them is not established, by anyone, yet.
  • Engines vary by region. If you sell internationally, run it from each market or accept that you've measured one.

Anyone selling you more certainty than that is selling you something.
